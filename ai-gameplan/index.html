AI Gameplan · A perspective for discussion

Thoughts on AI for a mid-sized California law firm

My view, from building and testing legal tools, of where AI could help a firm of about 200 lawyers across California. Leadership knows the firm far better than I do; this is meant to start a conversation.

In short

  1. Start with a clear policy and one approved tool. Most risk comes from people using unapproved tools because there is no good approved one.
  2. Lawyers decide; software checks. AI drafts and finds. Code verifies quotes, citations and numbers. A person approves everything that leaves the firm.
  3. Pick a few quick wins and measure them. Prebill guideline checks, citation checks before filing and conflicts party lists are good candidates.
  4. Match tools to data sensitivity. Client data only in approved enterprise tools; the most sensitive data only on the firm's own hardware.
  5. Longer term, consider one firm-owned platform that connects every system under the firm's own rules.

Based on public descriptions of a real California firm, which is not named. I don't know the firm's current tools or plans, and some of this may already be under way. Not legal or ethics advice.

1 · Why now

The rules are clear enough to act on, and clients are asking

  • Ethics guidance exists. The State Bar and ABA Opinion 512 map AI onto existing duties: competence, confidentiality, supervision, candor and fair fees.1,2
  • Courts are sanctioning mistakes. Noland (Cal. Ct. App. 2025): $10,000 for AI-fabricated citations.3,4
  • Clients are newly regulated. Employers (FEHA automated-decision rules), health care (AB 3030, SB 1120) and businesses (CCPA rules) all have AI questions, which is also an advisory opportunity.5,6,7

2 · Ground rules

Four rules I would start from

  1. A lawyer reviews all AI output. Judgment is never handed to a tool.1
  2. Verify before anything leaves the firm. Every citation, quote and number is checked against its source.
  3. Bill fairly. Actual time only; no charge for time saved or for learning a tool.1,2
  4. Measure, then scale. Pilot with a baseline before rolling anything out.

4 · Safeguards

Match each tool to how sensitive the data is

DataWhere it may go
PublicAny approved tool.
Firm internalEnterprise tools that don't train on firm data.
Client confidentialApproved enterprise tools with limited retention, single sign-on and audit logs, subject to client instructions.
Restricted (health data, walled matters)Only tools approved for it, or a model on the firm's own hardware.

Before buying any AI tool, get written answers on: training on firm data, retention, security certification, ethical walls, citations to sources, audit logs, and data return on exit.1

5 · A possible sequence

Four steps over about two years

One way to picture it; timing depends on what the firm already has.

  1. Month 1

    Foundations

    Decide who owns AI decisions, publish an interim policy, and survey what people already use.

  2. Months 1 to 3

    One safe tool

    Vet and license an assistant for a pilot group across offices; short training for everyone.

  3. Months 3 to 6

    A few measured pilots

    Run three or four quick wins against a baseline; scale, change or stop each.

  4. Months 6 to 24

    Connect and offer

    Connect proven workflows to firm systems, and consider AI governance advice for clients.

6 · A longer-term idea

One firm-owned platform

Eventually, one in-house layer could connect documents, email, conflicts, time, billing and docketing, with the firm's own permissions, walls and audit trail applied everywhere.

  • Why: control of client data, answers that draw on every system at once, and freedom to switch AI models.
  • What stays the same: existing systems remain the systems of record, and lawyers still decide.
  • How: build each quick win on shared parts, then connect systems one at a time, with a go/no-go at each step.

This is a direction to weigh, not a commitment to make now.

7 · Open questions

What I would want to know first

  1. What AI guidance or tools does the firm already have?
  2. Which groups feel the most pressure on time or write-offs?
  3. What are clients asking about AI?
  4. How much appetite is there to build versus buy?
  5. How should first-year success be judged?
