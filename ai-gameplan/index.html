AI Gameplan · A perspective for discussion

Thoughts on AI for a mid-sized California law firm

My perspective on where AI could help a full-service firm of about 200 lawyers with offices across California, drawn from building and testing legal tools. It sets out what I have observed, the ideas I think are worth exploring, one possible way to sequence them, and the questions I would want answered first. Leadership knows the firm far better than I do, so this is meant to start a conversation rather than settle one.

1 · Starting point

The firm and its markets

These ideas are written with this public profile in mind:

  • Size and footprint. Roughly 200 lawyers in several offices across Northern, Central and Southern California.
  • Practices. Public agencies and government, health care and senior care, construction, real estate, labor and employment, litigation, corporate, environment, intellectual property and family wealth planning.
  • Clients. Public agencies such as transit and water districts, health care and senior living organizations, construction and infrastructure owners, agriculture and mid-market companies, and families with significant wealth.
  • Values. A visible commitment to pro bono, inclusion and sustainability, including certification as a B Corporation.

Three features of this profile shape my thinking. Public-agency work means records, transparency and procurement rules apply to the clients, and sometimes to the work product. Health care work means protected health information is routinely in the file. And a statewide footprint means consistent training and tools matter more than in a single-office firm.

2 · Context

Why now is a good moment

As I see it, the question for a mid-sized firm is less whether lawyers will use AI than whether they use approved tools under a clear policy. Four developments make a deliberate approach timely.

Ethics guidance is clear enough to act on

The State Bar of California's practical guidance on generative AI and ABA Formal Opinion 512 (July 2024) both map AI use onto existing duties: competence, confidentiality, supervision, communication, candor and reasonable fees.1,2

Courts are raising the stakes

In Noland v. Land of the Free (Sept. 2025), the California Court of Appeal sanctioned counsel $10,000 for briefs with AI-fabricated citations and published the opinion as a warning. California's courts were also directed to adopt their own generative AI use policies by December 15, 2025.3,4

Clients are now regulated on AI

Employers face FEHA rules on automated decision systems (effective Oct. 1, 2025). Health care providers and plans face AB 3030 and SB 1120 (effective Jan. 1, 2025). Businesses face new CCPA rules on risk assessments (from Jan. 1, 2026) and automated decisionmaking (from Jan. 1, 2027).5,6,7

Clients are likely to ask about the firm's own use

Clients who are building their own AI governance can reasonably ask how their outside counsel handles AI and their data. A firm with a written policy, trained lawyers and vetted tools can answer that question well, and can advise on it.

The third point may also be a business opportunity: each of the firm's core client groups now has its own AI compliance questions, and the firm's lawyers already know those clients and regulators.

3 · Ground rules

Principles I would start from

Whatever the firm decides to do, these are the ground rules I have found matter most when building legal tools.

  1. Lawyers stay responsible. AI output is a starting point that a lawyer reviews; professional judgment is never delegated to a tool, as the State Bar guidance puts it.1
  2. Client data goes only where the policy allows. Confidential information goes only into tools the firm has approved for that level of data.
  3. Verify before anything leaves the firm. Every citation, quotation and number in a filing or client deliverable is checked against the source.
  4. Bill fairly. Lawyers bill for actual time spent prompting and reviewing, not for time the tool saved, and do not bill for learning a tool.1,2
  5. Measure, then scale. Each use is piloted with a baseline and a written result before it is rolled out.

5 · Governance

Thoughts on policy and governance

The State Bar guidance calls on managing lawyers to set clear policies on permissible use and to train lawyers and staff.1 The firm may already have this in hand. If not, one structure that could work for a firm this size:

An AI committee

A small standing group, for example: the general counsel or ethics partner (chair), the chief information or technology officer, the knowledge management lead, the director of finance or billing, two or three practice group representatives from different offices, and a paralegal or staff representative. It could approve tools, own the policy, pick pilots and report to firm leadership each quarter.

The AI use policy

Ideally short, written in plain language, and reviewed every six months. Topics I would expect it to cover:

  • Approved tools, each with the highest data level it may receive (see the data tiers below).
  • Prohibited uses, such as entering client information into consumer AI accounts, or using AI to make a final decision on conflicts, hiring or a client's legal position.
  • Verification, including the required citation and quotation check before any filing.
  • Client instructions, recorded per client and checked before use, since some clients restrict or require disclosure of AI use.
  • Disclosure, to clients where the use is significant, and to courts where a local rule or order requires it.
  • Billing, following the fee guidance: actual time for prompting and review, no charge for time saved or for learning a tool, and AI costs explained in engagement letters where they are charged.1,2
  • Incidents, with a simple way to report a mistake or a data exposure without blame.

6 · Protecting clients

Confidentiality and ethics

The State Bar guidance warns against entering confidential information into any tool that lacks adequate confidentiality and security protections, and says to check whether a provider uses inputs to train its models or shares them.1 One way to turn that into an everyday rule is a set of data tiers:

Data tierExamplesWhere it may go
PublicPublished cases, statutes, the firm's own published alerts.Any approved tool.
Firm internalForms, templates, internal policies, anonymized examples.Enterprise tools under a firm contract with no training on inputs.
Client confidentialMatter documents, client communications, deal terms.Enterprise tools approved for client data, with zero or limited retention, single sign-on and audit logs, and subject to client instructions.
RestrictedProtected health information, walled or highly confidential matters, sealed material, sensitive personal data.Only tools approved for that category, such as a vendor covered by a business associate agreement, or a model running on the firm's own hardware.

Other safeguards worth considering

  • Ethical walls apply to AI. A tool that searches across matters must respect the same walls as the document system, so a screened lawyer cannot reach walled material through an AI search.
  • Anonymize where possible. Remove names and identifying details when the task does not need them.
  • Bias and fairness. Avoid AI in decisions about people, such as hiring or client intake screening, unless the tool has been tested for bias, consistent with Rule 8.4.1 and the FEHA rules.1,5
  • Candor. Lawyers check every authority and correct any error promptly, as both the guidance and Noland require.1,3

7 · People

Ideas for training

The training that seems to work best is short, practical and tied to the policy, and it can count toward the competence duty. A possible shape:

AudienceContentFormat
EveryoneThe policy, the data tiers, approved tools, and how to report a problem.A 45-minute session and a one-page reference card, repeated for new hires.
LawyersEthics and verification: how output fails, citation and quote checking, billing rules and client instructions. Eligible for California ethics CLE credit if presented by an approved provider.A one-hour session, then practice-group sessions with real (anonymized) examples.
Practice championsPrompting, building reusable checklists and prompts, measuring results and coaching colleagues.One or two champions per practice and office, meeting monthly.
Staff and paralegalsWorkflow-specific training for intake, billing, docketing and document work.Hands-on sessions by team.

A shared prompt and checklist library, organized by practice, would help keep good methods from staying in one lawyer's head.

8 · Buying well

Vetting vendors

These are the questions I would want answered in writing, both for a new legal AI product and for AI features added to tools the firm already uses.

AreaQuestions to answer in writing
Data useAre inputs or outputs used to train models? How long are they kept, and can retention be set to zero? Who at the vendor or its subprocessors can see them?
SecurityCurrent SOC 2 Type II or ISO 27001 report; encryption at rest and in transit; single sign-on and user provisioning; where data is stored and processed.
Access and wallsDoes the tool inherit permissions from the document management system? Can it enforce ethical walls and matter-level restrictions?
AccuracyAre answers cited to sources the user can open? How does the vendor measure and report errors? Can the firm run its own test set before buying?
AuditAre prompts, outputs and user actions logged, and can the firm export the logs?
Regulated dataWill the vendor sign a business associate agreement? Does it meet any public-agency client requirements?
ContractConfidentiality terms, breach notice, indemnity, insurance, data return and deletion on exit, and notice before the underlying model changes.
Cost and fitPer-seat versus usage pricing, a pilot period, and integration with the systems the firm already runs.

9 · The toolkit

Tools worth evaluating

My view is that most capability should come from established products, and that a small number of firm-specific workflows may be worth building, because they encode how the firm works and sit on top of systems it already has.

Products to evaluate

  • A secure general assistant for drafting, summarizing and research support, licensed under an enterprise agreement with no training on firm data. Candidates include the enterprise editions of Claude, ChatGPT and Microsoft 365 Copilot; the right choice depends on the firm's existing Microsoft environment and the vetting above.
  • AI features in the firm's legal research service, because answers are grounded in the service's own citator and content.
  • AI-assisted review in the firm's e-discovery platform, which extends technology-assisted review the litigation group may already use.
  • AI search in the document management system, where it respects existing permissions and walls.
  • Transcription and meeting summaries for internal meetings, with participants' consent and the same data rules.

Workflows that might be worth building

Small tools that check the model with code and leave every decision to a person. I have built a working prototype of each, in this portfolio, so the ideas can be tried rather than imagined.

10 · Sequence

One possible sequence

If it helps to picture how the pieces could fit together, this is one way to sequence them. The timing and order would depend on what the firm already has in place and on leadership's priorities.

  1. Phase 0 · Month 1

    Foundations

    • Form the AI committee and name practice champions.
    • Issue an interim AI use policy with the data tiers.
    • Survey which AI tools people already use, without penalty, to see real demand.
    • Start vendor vetting for a secure general assistant.

    Done when: the interim policy is published and everyone has acknowledged it.

  2. Phase 1 · Months 1 to 3

    Safe general use

    • Roll out the approved assistant to a pilot group of 30 to 50 lawyers and staff across practices and offices.
    • Run all-hands and lawyer ethics training, and start the prompt library.
    • Require the citation and quotation check for filings.
    • Record baselines for the tasks to be piloted next.

    Done when: pilot users are active weekly, with no data incidents, and the policy is final.

  3. Phase 2 · Months 3 to 6

    Targeted practice pilots

    • Run three or four workflow pilots chosen by the committee, for example lease abstracts in real estate, BAA review in health care, deposition outlines in employment litigation, and the prebill guideline check in finance.
    • Measure each pilot against its baseline and write up the result.
    • Widen the general assistant to the whole firm.

    Done when: each pilot has a written result and a decision to scale, change or stop.

  4. Phase 3 · Months 6 to 12

    Firm systems

    • Connect proven workflows to the document management, conflicts, time and billing systems.
    • Add AI-assisted conflicts party lists and time-entry drafts, each with approval steps.
    • Stand up a private model on firm hardware for restricted data if the pilots show the need.
    • Review the policy with six months of experience.

    Done when: at least three workflows are in daily use with measured results.

  5. Phase 4 · Months 12 to 24

    Client value and advisory

    • Offer fixed-fee services where AI makes the work predictable, such as portfolio lease reviews or policy updates.
    • Package AI governance advice for the firm's core client groups: public agencies, health care and senior care organizations, and employers.
    • Share the firm's own governance approach with clients who ask.

    Done when: AI-supported services appear in pitches and client feedback is collected.

12 · Risks

Risks to plan for

RiskA possible response
Invented citations or quotationsA required citation and quotation check, then the citator, and training on how errors look.
Confidential data exposureData tiers, approved tools only, single sign-on and logs, and a no-blame incident process.
Unapproved toolsGive people a good approved tool early; in my experience most unapproved use comes from having none.
Billing disputesA clear billing rule and engagement-letter language, and alternative fees where AI makes work predictable.
Bias in decisions about peopleNo AI decisions on hiring or intake screening without testing and human review.
Vendor dependenceContracts with data export and model-change notice, and workflows that can switch models.
Uneven adoption across officesChampions in every office and practice, and pilots spread across offices.
Staff concern about rolesBe clear that the goal is better work and less drudgery, and involve staff in choosing pilots.

13 · Getting started

Possible first steps

If leadership wanted to move, these are the early steps I would suggest considering, roughly in this order:

  1. Decide who owns AI decisions, whether a committee or an existing group.
  2. Publish an interim policy and data tiers.
  3. Run a no-penalty survey of the AI tools people already use.
  4. Vet two or three general assistants against the vendor questions.
  5. License one for a pilot group across practices and offices.
  6. Hold a short policy session for everyone and an ethics session for lawyers.
  7. Require the citation and quotation check for filings.
  8. Record baselines for a few workflow pilots.
  9. Choose the pilots and report back to leadership.

14 · A longer-term idea

A longer-term idea: one firm platform

The ideas above add AI one workflow at a time. Looking further ahead, I think there is a strong case for eventually bringing those workflows together into a single in-house platform. It would connect every file, matter and system the firm uses, run under the firm's own control, and give every lawyer and staff member the same capabilities with the same safeguards. This is a direction to weigh, not a commitment to make now.

The idea in one sentence. One firm-owned layer that can read, search and act across documents, email, matters, conflicts, time, billing and docketing, with the firm's own permissions, walls and audit trail applied everywhere, and with models the firm chooses and can run on its own hardware.

Why it could be worth owning

  • Control of client data. Data stays in systems the firm runs or contracts for directly, under one set of rules, instead of spreading across a separate AI feature in every product.
  • Functionality across systems. A question like "what do we know about this company?" can draw on the conflicts database, the document system, billing history and email at once, which is hard to do inside any one vendor's product.
  • Built for this firm. Practice checklists, outside counsel guidelines, house style and the firm's own precedents become part of the tools.
  • Freedom to change models. Because the platform sits between the firm and the models, the firm can switch providers, or move sensitive work to private models, without rebuilding its workflows.
  • One governance model. Policy, data tiers, logging and review are enforced once, in the platform, rather than configured separately in each product.

What it would include

LayerWhat it does
ConnectorsRead and, where approved, write to the document management system, email, the conflicts and intake system, time and billing, docketing, the CRM and the intranet, through each system's supported interfaces.
One firm indexA secure, searchable index of documents and records, kept current as systems change, tagged by client, matter and data tier.
Permissions and wallsEvery request runs as the person asking, so the platform only sees what that person may see in the source systems, and ethical walls apply to search, answers and summaries alike.
Model gatewayRoutes each task to an approved model by data tier: private models on firm hardware for restricted data, and vetted enterprise models for the rest, with cost and usage tracked.
VerificationShared checks every tool uses: quotations matched to sources, citations checked against real opinions, dates and totals computed in code.
Workflow toolsThe practice and operations tools from the earlier phases (conflicts party lists, prebill checks, time drafts, lease and BAA reviews, deposition outlines, matter questions), all built on the same layers.
Audit and oversightA tamper-evident log of who asked what, which sources were used, which model answered and who approved the result, with reports for the AI committee.

What does not change

  • The existing document, billing, conflicts and docketing systems stay the systems of record. The platform connects to them; it does not replace them.
  • Lawyers still decide. The platform drafts, finds and checks, and every filing, clearance, entry and client communication is approved by a person.
  • Buying stays an option for each layer. Where a vendor product meets the vetting checklist and fits the platform, the firm can adopt it instead of building.

How it could come together

  1. Years 1 to 2 · During phases 2 to 4

    Build the workflows on shared parts

    Each workflow from the phased plan uses the same permission checks, verification and logging, so that the pieces of the platform already exist when the tools are joined up.

  2. Years 2 to 3

    Connect the core systems

    Build the firm index and connectors for the document system and the conflicts and matter data first, then time, billing and email. Add the model gateway and private models for restricted data.

  3. Year 3 and beyond

    One front door

    Give everyone a single place to ask questions about a matter, run any approved workflow and see results with sources, inside the tools they already use, such as Outlook, Word and the document system.

What it would take

  • A small permanent team from IT, knowledge management and practice support, with outside help for security reviews.
  • Infrastructure for the index and private models, on firm hardware or a private cloud tenancy the firm controls.
  • Vendor cooperation, since connectors depend on each system's supported interfaces and contract terms.
  • Security from the start, including penetration testing, access reviews and an incident plan before any connector goes live.
  • A decision gate at each stage, so the next layer is built only when the last one has shown measured value.

15 · Open questions

Open questions, and where I can help

There is a lot I can't see from outside the firm's leadership. These are the questions I would want answered before anything above is taken further:

  1. What AI guidance or tools does the firm already have, and how is it going?
  2. Which practice groups feel the most pressure on time, write-offs or response deadlines?
  3. What are clients asking about AI, in RFPs, outside counsel guidelines or conversations?
  4. Which systems would the firm never want an AI tool to touch, and why?
  5. How much appetite is there for building in-house, compared with buying?
  6. Who would leadership want to own AI decisions?
  7. How should success be judged in the first year: time saved, risk reduced, client value, or something else?
  8. Are there client or regulatory constraints, such as public-agency or health care terms, that rule options in or out?

What I can bring to the conversation

  • Working prototypes. Each tool idea above has a demo in this portfolio that can be opened and tried, which makes it easier to judge an idea on what it does rather than how it sounds.
  • Testing against real outcomes. I build tools to be measured against real results before anyone relies on them, and I'm glad to help set up a fair test of any tool the firm is considering.
  • Plain-language explanation. I'm happy to walk anyone through how these tools work, where they fail, and what safeguards they need.
